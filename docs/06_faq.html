6  FAQ

This section addresses common questions that may arise when assessing plastic emissions and their potential environmental impacts in LCA. The questions cover the scope and applicability of the assessment, the estimation of plastic emissions in the life cycle inventory, the selection and use of characterization factors, and the interpretation of results.

The answers provide practical guidance and, where relevant, refer to the corresponding sections and tables of this guide for further detail.

6.1 Scope and applicability

NoteDo all LCA studies need to include plastic emissions?

No. Whether plastic emissions should be included depends on the goal and scope of the study, the materials involved, the relevance of the flows, and the feasibility of the assessment. Section 2 proposes screening questions to support this decision. No single answer justifies including or excluding plastic emissions; the questions are meant to structure the practitioner’s reasoning.

NoteCan the plastic footprint be used as a standalone indicator?

Yes, the plastic footprint at the impact level can be used as a standalone indicator to compare plastic alternatives based on their potential impacts on the environment. It is nevertheless recommended to include it as part of an LCA covering all relevant impact categories, in order to avoid regrettable substitutions. For example, replacing a conventional polymer by a biodegradable one could lower the plastic footprint while increasing climate change impacts (Section 3.3.1).

NoteAre impacts on human health, on the carbon cycle, or from macroplastic entanglement and ingestion covered?

Not yet, in this edition of the guide. CFs for human toxicity (non-cancer) and for the marine biological carbon pump are under development and expected in 2027 (Section 1.2.4). CFs for macroplastic entanglement (Høiberg et al., 2024) and an effect factor for ingestion (Marhoon et al., 2026) exist but are not fully aligned with the modeling approach used for MiP (Sections 1.2.2.2 and 1.2.4.3).

NotePlastic emission inventory

Why are plastic emissions missing from my LCI database, and what about the background system? Even when an LCIA method includes the CFs, the corresponding elementary flows for plastic litter emissions are generally not available, or not systematically populated, in commonly used LCI databases (Section 2). Plastic emissions must therefore be quantified separately, mainly for the foreground system. Background systems will essentially not include plastic emissions, which is a limitation that should be acknowledged in the interpretation (Section 3.1.8).

NoteWhich data sources can I use to estimate plastic emissions?

The main sources currently available are the Plastic Footprint Network (PFN) technical modules, the Plastic Leak Project (PLP) sectorial guidance, and national statistics or country-specific assessments (Section 4, Tables 4.1 and 4.2). At the time of writing, three of the seven PFN modules are complete: packaging, textiles, and tire abrasion. Practitioners should check what each data source expresses (e.g., direct emissions or emissions after redistribution) before using it (Section 3.1.3).

NoteCan plastic mismanagement rates be used as emission factors?

No. Plastic mismanagement and plastic leakage to the environment are not synonymous. Mismanagement often includes open burning and landfilling, which are not direct emissions of plastic to the environment, although they may lead to leakage. Data that clearly identify leakage to the environment are needed (Section 3.1.3).

NoteHow do I choose the emission compartment when it is not known?

As a pragmatic default, the generic PFN release rates for waterways and land can be used to allocate emissions to continental sea water and continental natural soil, respectively. For microplastic emissions from textiles, emissions to land should be assigned to agricultural soil. Fractions assumed to be removed or cleaned should not be characterized as emissions, and the assumptions made should be documented (Section 3.1.4 and Table 3.5).

NoteThe CFs are only available for microplastics. How should macroplastic emissions be handled?

The fraction of MaP emissions that fragments into MiP is estimated using fragmentation rates that depend on the material type (rigid or flexible) and on the emission compartment (Table 3.7) and is then characterized with the CFs. The remaining fraction is currently not characterized, which leads to an underestimation of impacts. A sensitivity analysis on the fragmentation rate is therefore recommended (Sections 3.1.5, 3.1.8, and 3.2.2).

6.2 Characterization factors and LCIA

NoteWhat type of effects are accounted for in the physical effects on biota characterization factors?

The CFs represent the physical effects of microplastic (MiP) particles on biota, namely entanglement, ingestion, and adherence, which may lead to mortality or to reduced growth, feeding, or reproduction (Section 3.2.1). They are derived from ecotoxicological experiments using virgin particles and therefore do not include the chemical effects of additives (Sections 1.2.3 and 3.2.2). They are available for freshwater, marine, and terrestrial ecosystems and are expressed under the area of protection Ecosystem Quality.

NoteWhere to find the most up-to-date set of characterization factors?

The PEBBLE CFs (Saadi et al., 2026) are the most up-to-date set of CFs for physical effects on biota. They are implemented in IMPACT World+ and GLAM (Section 3.2.1), and the free online tool that accompanies this guide facilitates the calculations. Future CFs developed by the MarILCA working group will be added to this guide and its tool as they become available (Section 1.2.4).

NoteWhich particle shape and size should I use when they are unknown?

Unspecified shapes are considered spherical (Table 3.9). Default sizes by shape are provided in Table 3.11: 1000 µm for spheres, 10 µm for fibers, and 100 µm for film fragments, with a range of 1000 to 5000 µm for unspecified shapes. For film-like MaP, the thickness of the item can be used to estimate the size of the resulting MiP. Because CFs vary considerably with particle size, this choice should be documented and tested (Sections 3.1.6 and 3.1.7).

NoteIn which units are the CFs provided, and which ones should I use?

The CFs are provided at midpoint and damage levels, in PDF.m2.yr/kg for IMPACT World+, PDF.yr/kg for GLAM, and species.yr/kg for ReCiPe. The units to use are those of the LCIA method applied to the other impact categories of the study, so that results can be combined and compared (Section 3.2.1.1).

NoteWhat is the difference between short-term and long-term CFs, and which ones should I use?

The short-term CFs quantify the potential impacts occurring during the first 100 years, and the long-term CFs those occurring beyond 100 years, similarly to the short-term and long-term Climate change indicators of IMPACT World+ (Section 3.2.1). As for climate change, this choice reflects a value choice regarding the time perspective and should be stated transparently in the goal and scope of the study.

NoteAre the impacts of chemical additives included?

No. The current CFs only account for the physical effects of polymer particles. When the identity, amount, release, fate, and toxicity of additives are known, their impacts can be assessed through existing ecotoxicity impact pathways (Sections 1.2.3 and 3.2.2). Current evidence suggests that most potential impacts on Ecosystem Quality are associated with the polymer fraction rather than with the additives (Lavoie et al., under review).

6.3 Interpretation

NoteMy results seem incoherent, what should I look for specifically when revising my assessment?

The most frequent sources of inconsistency are the following: (1) units, as emitted masses must be expressed in kg and the CF units must match the LCIA method used (Section 3.2.1.1); (2) emission factors, which must represent emissions at the point of release and not mismanagement rates or redistribution rates, to avoid double-counting fate (Section 3.1.3); (3) the region and emission compartment assigned to each flow (Sections 3.1.2 and 3.1.4); (4) the fragmentation of macroplastics (MaP), as only the fragmenting fraction can be characterized (Section 3.1.5); (5) the particle shape and size, since CFs can vary by up to six orders of magnitude across sizes (Sections 3.1.6 and 3.1.7); and (6) the choice between short-term and long-term CFs (Section 3.2.1).

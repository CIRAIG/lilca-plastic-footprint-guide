3  How to assess potential biodiversity impacts of plastic emissions in LCAs

The Ecosystem Quality (EQ) area of protection addresses potential impacts on ecosystems and biodiversity. In this section, the CFs for MiP are used to assess these potential impacts within the LCA framework.

The following sections, designed as a step-by-step procedure, will allow practitioners to fully understand and apply the CFs for plastic emissions presented in this guide.

3.1 Introducing the illustrative example

To exemplify the complete process, this section uses a continuous example. In this example, a comparative LCA is conducted to assess the environmental performance of five drinking containers used in quick-service restaurant (QSR) settings:

  1. Reusable ceramic mug provided by the QSR
  2. Reusable PP cup provided by the QSR
  3. Reusable PP travel container brought by the customer
  4. Single-use PLA-lined paper cup provided by the QSR
  5. Single-use LDPE-lined paper cup provided by the QSR
  6. Single-use PET cup provided by the QSR
  7. Single-use PP cup provided by the QSR

To allow the consumption of a single medium-sized cold beverage (~355 ml) in QSR in Montréal, Québec, Canada in 2026.

3.2 Life cycle inventory for the foreground system

The following steps allow the computation of an inventory of plastic emissions to the environment over the life cycle of the product or service considered. At the inventory stage, details are needed on the emission country and emission compartment, the polymer type, the shape, and the size.

3.2.1 Processes and materials

As a first step in your assessment, the processes in which emissions of plastic pieces or particles can occur must be identified. Here, the focus should be on the foreground system, for reasons mentioned in the previous sections. The processes typically associated with plastic leakage are likely to be similar to the modules identified in the PFN or the PLP. For all types of plastic products, emissions are indeed likely to occur during plastic granulate manufacturing, road transportation with tire abrasion and end-of-life management, including littering. Some processes are associated with specific sectors. For example, the washing of garments and textiles during the use stage is a process in which microfibers are emitted to wastewater. The same reasoning applies to the fishing industry, which can lose fishing gear at sea, and agricultural practices involving mulch films.

For end-of-life processes, it is also important to highlight that leakage could occur via multiple pathways. Littered, uncollected and improperly disposed waste may all end up in natural environments but differ greatly in proportions. Also, waste can be managed suboptimally without necessarily leaking entirely to the environment. This distinction is relevant and justifies the need for a precise identification of processes involved in the targeted systems during the assessment. Some plastic emissions can also originate from waste exports. Indeed, waste mismanagement that occurs once exported waste lands in a country with inefficient waste management infrastructure could also be taken into consideration, if data allow this leakage to be quantified. This is the case for many high-income countries that export a significant fraction of their plastic waste to low- or middle-income countries that rely on inadequate waste management schemes (Kounina et al. 2024).

The identification of the processes involved in the plastic emissions should allow practitioners to also identify the polymer composition of the emissions, which constitutes relevant information since CFs are available per plastic (e.g., PET, HDPE and PP) or other material (e.g., cotton and viscose). This composition of emissions is directly related to the composition of the materials assessed. Therefore, when conducting the environmental assessment of a PET food container, for example, the emissions will consist of PET.

3.2.2 Geographical location

The geographic location is a key parameter of plastic leakage and impacts. First, the location will affect emission rates of plastic into the environment. In fact, countries and regions with better waste management systems and high-efficiency wastewater treatment plants (WWTP) will lead to smaller emissions of MaP and MiP into the environment, compared to locations with poor waste management and WWTPs. As a result, the same item used in different contexts can lead to different emissions of plastic into the environment.

Second, the location also influences CFs as the fate and effects of MaP and MiP on biota can be regionalized. So far, CFs are therefore available for the following regions: Africa & Middle East, Central Asia, Europe, Latin America, North America, Northern Regions, Oceania, Southeast Asia, and a default World region, following USEtox regionalization (Figure 3.1 shows the available regions). Regional differentiation accounts for parameters such as rainfall rate, water body depth and area, species richness, area fraction, and runoff, all of which influence the environmental fate and ultimate impact of MiP.

Practitioners should therefore identify the country and geographic region in which the processes and activities under study occur, as this determines which regional leakage rate and regional CF values to apply. For instance, road transport activities taking place during distribution stages in North America should be characterized using the North America CF values and emission rates. This same logic applies to manufacturing processes, which may represent another source of MiP emissions and can occur across multiple regions within a single product system. When the location of emission cannot be determined, the World region can be used as the default.

3.2.3 Emission quantity

Following the identification of processes involved in plastic emissions throughout the life cycle of the product system under study, the quantification of these processes must be undertaken. The data used for this purpose may be labeled as emission factors, loss rates, leakage rates, or release rates, and communicates the quantity of plastic (expressed as mass) emitted by a process per unit of activity or per unit of material, for example. Note that mass is currently the designated metric of interest for quantifying plastic emissions. Particle count is also recognized as an important metric in MiP science, particularly for toxicity assessment (Thornton Hampton et al. 2022); however, mass remains the only unit used in the LCA field to date. Accordingly, any data expressed in particle numbers must be converted to mass using information on particle size and density before use in an LCA context. Lavoie et al. (2022) and Besseling et al. (2019) provide worked examples of such conversions.

Various conventions are used throughout the scientific and industrial literature to express quantities of emitted plastics. Emission factors are frequently expressed as percentages or fractions, representing the proportion of a plastic material considered to be emitted to the environment, but may also be expressed as a mass of plastic per unit of process or activity. Some of the variation observed in units and terminology is attributable to differences in what is being quantified. For instance, in the dataset published by the PFN (Plastic Footprint Guidelines 2023), some release rates (i.e., RelR) quantify direct environmental emissions, whereas others (i.e., RR) quantify emissions reaching specific environmental compartments following release and subsequent redistribution – whether through natural processes (e.g., transport of MiP from freshwater to oceans via currents) or human interventions (e.g., wastewater treatment). It is therefore essential for practitioners to carefully examine what each data source is expressing and quantifying before use. Some data sources are provided in Section 4 of this guide.

a Emissions to oceans from a t-shirt wash cycle in Canada are calculated by multiplying the reported loss rate (LR) and release rate (RRocean).

It is important to note that plastic mismanagement and plastic leakage to the environment are not synonymous, and therefore, plastic mismanagement rates cannot directly be used to determine the amount of plastic emitted to the environment. In fact, plastic mismanagement often encompasses open burning and landfilling which are not direct environmental emissions of plastic, but which may lead to environmental leakage of plastic. Data clearly identifying leakage to the environment is needed for this assessment.

A related and critical issue concerns the risk of double-counting fate when estimating quantities of plastic emissions to natural environments. As noted above, emission factors are intended to represent the mass of plastics emitted to the environment at the point of release (initial environmental compartment), while the fate modeling in the CFs accounts for redistribution between compartments. In certain methodological frameworks, however – including the PFN (Plastic Footprint Guidelines 2023) and the PLP (Peano et al. 2020) – additional redistribution rates are also provided to account for the subsequent transport of particles between environmental compartments. This creates an inconsistency when these data are combined with CFs for physical effects on biota, since CFs already fully account for the fate of MiP once released to natural compartments. Practitioners drawing on both of these data sources and the physical effects on biota CFs must therefore ensure that only emission values into the initial environmental compartment (i.e., the first environmental compartment that microplastics reach from the technosphere) – and not any further redistribution rates – are carried forward into the impact assessment.

3.2.4 Environmental compartment

Impacts are emission compartment-dependent and are available for the following emission compartments: air, agricultural soil, natural soil, river water, lake water, and sea water (see Table 3.6). In practice, the emission compartment may be straightforward to identify for some processes, but difficult to determine precisely for others.

Precise compartment assignment requires detailed information on where plastic emissions occur and, where relevant, how leaked plastic is transported before reaching the receiving environment. This level of detail is often unavailable to practitioners. Ideally, activity-specific leakage models would provide this information in a similar way as LCI datasets provide elementary flows for a wide range of substances and activities. However, plastic emissions are not yet systematically represented in these data sources. For now, practitioners therefore need to rely on complementary data sources and transparent assumptions.

At the time of writing, the most useful source of information on this matter is provided by the PFN technical modules and accompanying spreadsheets. The release rates in each module include the share of leaked plastic assigned to different receiving compartments. In the PFN data sheets used for this guide, the granularity and completeness of release rates vary by sector (see modules for complete explanation).

For example, in the Macroplastic: Packaging spreadsheet, the “Leaked to Ocean and Waterways” column provides country-specific release rates for a combined marine and freshwater compartment. This helps estimate releases to aquatic environments, but does not distinguish between sea water, river water, and lake water, and therefore does not allow direct selection of the most specific CF. In the same spreadsheet, additional generic release rates are provided for ocean and land, depending on the size of the plastic item emitted and its residual value. Larger items and items with higher residual value are associated with lower release rates. These release rates may not sum to 100%, because the remaining fraction is assumed to be removed, for example through waste picking or municipal cleaning operations. In other modules, such as the textile microfiber module, leakage is distinguished between freshwater and marine environments.

In case no precise environmental compartments are available for the system studied, as a pragmatic default, it is recommended to first apply the generic release rates for “waterways” and land of the PFN to allocate the emitted fraction between continental sea water and continental natural soil, respectively. Alternatively, depending on the country, practitioners may assume that the “Leaked to Ocean and Waterways” fraction is emitted to sea water and that the remaining emitted fraction is released to land, represented here as natural soil. This simplified compartment assignment should be documented, and fractions assumed to be removed or cleaned should not be characterized as emissions.

For MiP emissions from textile production and use, emissions to land should be assigned to agricultural soil since the release pathway occurs via sewage sludge spreading on agricultural fields. Recommendations on compartment assignment are provided in Table 3.6.

For MiP emissions from tire abrasion, it is strongly recommended to use the specific CFs available in Ospital et al. (2026), where applicable, as these incorporate a weighting based on the likelihood of emissions reaching different natural compartments at the continental scale. Further parameterization is possible using PFN data to adapt the emission distribution according to watershed size and the share of driving occurring in rural, urban, and highway contexts. Because updates to the PFN methodology and data are expected, these recommendations should be revisited when new versions become available.

For compartments represented in the fate model in both continental and global versions, emissions should by default be assigned to the continental compartment. This recommendation may be revised when process-specific information justifies an alternative assignment. For instance, emissions from fishing operations conducted in open ocean waters may appropriately be assigned to the global sea water compartment. In any case, CF values between continental and global versions of a given compartment are expected to differ only marginally and are unlikely to materially affect assessment results.

3.2.5 Fragmentation

For many of the emitting processes identified previously, the plastic emissions will take the form of MaPs. Yet, CFs for physical effects on biota are only available for micro-sized plastic emissions. Secondary microplastics, which originate from the fragmentation of larger plastics, are nevertheless thought to be the most abundant form of microplastics in comparison with primary microplastics, manufactured as such (Mutuku et al. 2024). This means that MaPs undergo significant fragmentation under forces encountered in the environment. A significant proportion of MaPs that end up in natural environments are going to be fragmented over time, especially over long-term horizons.

On this matter, a few recommendations can be put forward, based on a peer-reviewed degradation and fragmentation model (Tang et al., 2026). A value in percentage (%) is set to consider that only a proportion of MaP emissions are going to be fragmented and should thus be characterized using the CFs for physical effects on biota. Those fragmentation percentages vary depending on material type (i.e., rigid or flexible), and emission compartment (e.g., topsoil, beach or water surface). Since degradation and fragmentation are competing processes on MaPs, the remainder of the MaP that does not fragment will degrade during the same time horizon. The distinction is made here between flexible and rigid plastics, with the hypothesis that a flexible plastic is thinner than a rigid one, leading to the flexible material having a surface area to volume ratio (SA:V) that is larger than that of a rigid polymer, and thus making it more susceptible to fragmentation (Tang et al., 2026). Plastics thicker than 250 µm can be considered rigid (low SA:V), while plastics thinner than 250 µm can be considered flexible (high SA:V). Degradation and fragmentation processes are functions of environmental conditions, which depend on the environmental compartments, namely UV intensity and mechanical forces. These recommendations should be refined as more granular fragmentation data become available for different polymers (e.g., distinguishing between flexible PET and PP). Additionally, the remaining fraction of plastic that is not assumed to fragment and will remain as MaP until it degrades (albeit passing through a MiP size at some point) cannot be characterized using the physical effects on biota CFs which are for MiP only and therefore remains uncharacterized at the moment in the absence of consistent indicators expressing its potential contribution to damages on biodiversity.

3.2.6 Shape

The shape of emitted particles is generally determined by the type of product. Detailed recommendations on this matter, drawn from Corella-Puertas et al. (2023), are provided in this guide and summarized in Table 3.13, as follows: fragments from film-like materials (wrappings, bags, etc.) should be characterized as film fragments; particles from fiber-containing materials (textiles, ropes, etc.) should be treated as fibers; and particles originating from foams or foam-like materials, as well as microbeads, should be considered spherical. For particles of unknown or unspecified shape, the default assumption is also spherical – a choice supported by evidence that plastic fragmentation tends to produce increasingly rounded particles over time (Alkema et al. 2022). It should be noted that the shape of the MiP has been shown to have little influence on the impacts (Hajjar et al. 2026; Louvet et al. 2026; Saadi and Boulay 2026).

3.2.7 Size

CFs expressing damage to biodiversity from MiP emissions span up to six orders of magnitude across the 1–5000 µm size range (Louvet et al. 2026; Saadi and Boulay 2026). Particle size is therefore a critical parameter in LCA, as the choice of size class can significantly influence results. For primary MiP – particles manufactured and released directly at the micro scale – size characterization is generally straightforward, as their dimensions are defined at the point of production and should be known to the practitioner.

The size distribution profile of microplastics generated from fragmentation of macro-sized litter is highly uncertain.

In this context, guidance is provided to practitioners to support that decision. Based on the commonness of microplastic sizes found in the environment and the industry, Corella-Puertas et al. (2023) propose default sizes for the various shapes of microplastics. For generic plastic materials (MaPs) (e.g., buoys, plastic packaging, and toys) that have to be characterized as microplastics in order to use the MiP-oriented CFs, the recommended size class is 1000 µm or 5000 µm since large particles will likely dominate the fragmented mass during the early stages of the fragmentation process (Egger et al. 2020; Kaandorp et al. 2021; Ueda et al. 2025). This also matches the recommendations from Corella-Puertas et al. (2023) for fragments of unspecified shape. For wrappings, bags and other film-like materials, the thickness of the MaP itself can be used to estimate the size of resulting MiP (e.g., CFs for 10 µm films could be used for an 8 µm-thick LDPE bag). This information is presented in Table 3.15 below.

For clarity, it should be noted that fiber and film fragment sizes refer to their diameter and thickness, respectively – not to particle length or overall dimensions. These dimensional properties govern environmental fate and therefore constitute the primary characteristics of interest in this analysis. For spheres or unknown shape fragments, the diameter represents the dimension of interest.

Combining all the previous steps, the mass of MiP of a given polymer, shape, and size emitted to each environmental compartment is calculated as follows (Equation 3.1):

\[ E_{i,c} = M_i \times EF_i \times R_{i,c} \times FR_{i,c} \tag{3.1}\]

where \(E_{i,c}\) is the mass of MiP (kg) emitted by process \(i\) to compartment \(c\); \(M_i\) is the mass of plastic (kg) involved in process \(i\); \(EF_i\) is the emission factor of process \(i\) (fraction of \(M_i\) emitted to the environment); \(R_{i,c}\) is the fraction of the emitted mass released to compartment \(c\) (release rate); and \(FR_{i,c}\) is the fragmentation rate, i.e., the fraction of the emitted MaP that fragments into MiP in compartment c (\(FR_{i,c} = 1\) for primary MiP emissions).

3.2.8 Limits of the inventory of plastic emissions

Limits and weaknesses of plastic emission inventories exist and are worth highlighting since they can significantly influence results of the assessment.

Drawing on experience from previous works estimating plastic emissions to natural environments, the most pressing limit of the inventory relates to the fragmentation of MaPs into secondary microplastics, which are then characterized using CFs. Different assumptions have been made in the past (10%, 50% and 100% in Corella-Puertas et al. (2023); UNEP, 2022; 0.5% in Erradhouani et al. (2026); 100% in Louvet et al. (2026); 4%, 12% and 62% in Saadi et al. (2025)), and uncertainty remains on the fragmentation percentage calculated via PlasticFADE (Tang et al., 2026), which reveals the important unknowns associated with this parameter, especially when considering the variety of product types, environmental conditions and polymer chemistry involved, including additives affecting plasticity and fragmentation potential. We therefore recommend always including a sensitivity analysis on the value of this parameter.

A further limitation relates to the focus on the foreground system. Since plastic emissions inventories are not currently assessed in LCI databases, background systems will essentially never include plastic emissions, even though in reality some occur in background packaging, road transport and oceanic freight activities, for example.

Most limits faced during the inventory stage will likely originate from data availability. Some sectors may be unable to find accurate and reliable data on the plastic emissions occurring throughout their supply chain. Because data may come from many sources, methodologies and assumptions may also differ, inducing uncertainty in the assessment. Furthermore, obtaining uncertainty estimates (e.g., confidence intervals) for inventory emission factors is challenging given the current state of knowledge in the field.

Specific limitations are also linked with data sources or methodologies. For example, in low-income countries (LIC) or middle-income countries (MIC), open dumps are the de facto treatment for waste disposal. Yet, some methodologies such as the PFN (Plastic Footprint Guidelines 2023) and the PLP (Peano et al. 2020) consider this end-of-life treatment as waste mismanagement and apply a 100% leakage to litter taking this path. There is consensus that open dumps are far from optimal waste management schemes. Yet, using 100% stands in stark contrast to other scientific studies, which instead suggest rates of leakage ranging from 0.04% (Cottom et al. 2024) and 5% (Schwarz et al. 2023) to 25% (OECD 2022).

Similarly, potential plastic emissions resulting from plastic waste exports are also currently not accounted for directly by emission factors provided by the PFN (Plastic Footprint Guidelines 2023). In fact, the share of plastic waste exports is provided, but linking these exports to specific countries and assigning the respective emission factors of waste treated in that country requires additional data and work by the practitioner. It is still possible to proceed to the quantification of emissions resulting from plastic waste exports. To do so, practitioners should rely on waste export shares provided for the country where the end-of-life of their product system is occurring, which is given in the PFN data. Then, identifying the countries of destination for the waste exports would be ideal, but it is also possible to use average waste mismanagement percentages for low and/or middle-income countries provided in the PLP data, and multiply the two values to estimate the resulting emission factors. Then, the release calculation step can be followed normally to identify the emission compartment distribution values.

3.3 Life cycle impact assessment (LCIA)

The previous section introduced the steps needed to obtain life cycle emissions of plastics, in terms of polymer, mass, size, shape and location. Yet, this information alone is insufficient to represent impacts of plastic pollution, as it does not reflect environmental damages associated with the different types of emissions. The following section links the inventory to impact assessment via the use of characterization factors. To generate an impact score, emissions (mass) must be multiplied by the corresponding CFs (impact per mass), for the relevant impact category (e.g., physical effects) and for the LCIA method of interest (IMPACT World+, GLAM, or ReCiPe).

Formally, the impact score is calculated as follows (Equation 3.2):

\[ I = \sum_{i,c} E_{i,c} \times CF_{i,c} \tag{3.2}\]

where \(I\) is the impact score (e.g., in PDF.m2.yr for IMPACT World+), \(E_{i,c}\) is the mass of MiP emitted by process \(i\) to compartment \(c\) (kg emitted) (Equation 3.2), and \(CF_{i,c}\) is the corresponding CF (impact per kg emitted), which depends on the polymer, shape, size, region, and emission compartment of the flow \(E_{i,c}\). Currently, only the physical effects on biota CFs are fully operationalizable, but this guide will be updated as additional impact categories are available for use in LCIA.

3.3.1 Physical effects on biota

The PEBBLE CFs for physical effects on biota express the potential detrimental impacts on ecosystem quality associated with emissions of MiP to natural environments (see Section 1.2.2.1 for more information). They account for both the fate of MiP across environmental compartments and potential effects of MiP on exposed organisms. They are available for two time scales, representing short-term and long-term perspectives offered by different time-horizon perspectives, similar to the Climate change short-term and long-term indicators available in the IMPACT World+ methodology. Using the same approach as Climate change, the short-term physical effects on biota CFs quantify the potential impacts occurring during the 0–100 years time horizon, while long-term CFs quantify those occurring beyond 100 years.

As indicated by the name of the impact category, the current CFs account only for physical effects. This reflects the way the effect factors were developed: they rely on ecotoxicological data from experiments using virgin MiP, in order to isolate the physical effects of plastic particles on organisms, without including potential effects from additives or other chemicals present in plastic product matrices (Lavoie et al. 2022; Corella-Puertas et al. 2022, 2023; Saadi et al. 2025; Louvet et al. 2026; Saadi and Boulay 2026). Chemical substances added to plastic products to modify or enhance their properties, such as additives, are therefore not represented in the physical effects on biota pathway. When relevant and when sufficient inventory and substance-specific data are available, these chemical contributions should instead be considered through existing ecotoxicity impact pathways. Similarly, the fate modeling focuses on the intrinsic properties of the polymer particles, for example degradation and fragmentation behavior, and does not explicitly account for formulation-specific effects such as additional stability induced by additives. Sections 1.2.3 and 3.2.2 discuss the distinction between physical effects on biota and ecotoxicity, and proposes approaches for addressing this issue in practice.

The physical effects considered in this pathway include entanglement and ingestion. These mechanisms may lead to adverse outcomes such as mortality, reduced growth, reduced feeding, or reduced reproduction. The CFs are provided for 23 materials, 5 sizes, 3 shapes, 10 environmental compartments and 2 scales (Table 3.19). The CFs can be found in Saadi et al. (2026), and the model limitations are discussed in the underlying publication.

The potential environmental impacts of MiP can be assessed separately per ecosystem (i.e., impacts on freshwater, marine, and terrestrial ecosystems), as CFs are provided separately for each. Note that impacts account for MiP transfer between ecosystems, meaning that emissions to terrestrial compartments lead to freshwater and marine impact as well. It is also possible to sum the impacts on all three ecosystems if needed to get a total impact on EQ (and CFs for the total ecosystem impacts are also provided in PEBBLE).

3.3.1.1 Units for various modeling frameworks and LCIA methodologies

Various methodologies are available for practitioners to proceed with the LCIA step of their assessment. These LCIA methodologies combine different models for a number of impact categories and sometimes also imply a series of value choices in their modeling assumptions (e.g., time horizons considered). LCIA methods are considered to be either midpoint or damage oriented, while some methods also cover both (Bulle et al. 2019). The PEBBLE method for the assessment of physical effects on biota impact category provides CFs for both midpoint and damage levels as well and is implemented in IMPACT World+ and GLAM (Bulle et al. 2019; Verones et al. 2026). To facilitate the integration of the CFs for physical effects on biota with diverse LCIA methodologies, they are provided for different LCIA methods in different units, namely IMPACT World+ (in PDF.m2.yr/kg), GLAM (PDF.yr/kg), and ReCiPe (species.yr/kg) (Saadi et al. 2026).

3.3.2 Limits of the impact assessment of plastic emissions

Models are incomplete representations of reality and therefore hold numerous limitations. This section discusses the current limitations for the impact assessment of plastic emissions. First, the physical effects on biota CFs are for now only available for MiP-sized plastic particles. Combining this with the uncertainty related to environmental fragmentation processes and the absence of CFs for MaP means that significant fractions of the plastic litter emitted to the environment may not be accounted for in the impact assessment step, specifically for rigid plastics due to their lower fragmentation rates. This results in a systematic underestimation of impacts and creates a mass balance inconsistency: the total mass of plastic recorded in the life cycle inventory is not fully characterized at the impact assessment level, as MaPs are effectively assigned a zero-impact weight by

Second, current modeling is limited to the physical effects on biota. This implies that plastic pieces leaking to the environment are assumed to be composed of 100% polymer chains, rather than a mix of polymers and other substances including loose monomers, chemical additives and non-intentionally added substances (NIAS). This omission is explained by the large variety of substances and degrees to which they are present in the different plastic compositions, thus limiting the possibility to provide generic CFs. This might however not be as significant for the impact assessment of plastic litter according to a recent study (Lavoie et al., under review). In that study, authors showed that available additive content for some food-contact plastic items had only a marginal impact on ecosystems (via ecotoxicity impact pathway) compared to the physical effect of the secondary MiP emitted during the fragmentation of the items (Lavoie et al., under review). A dedicated practitioner could include impacts of additives following the method outlined in Lavoie et al. (under review) if additive composition is known or can be extrapolated.

Third, limitations relating to the modeling of the physical effects on biota are reported in the respective papers. They include, but are not limited to (1) the use of a single generic effect factor to represent the physical effect of particles ranging from 0.001 µm to 5000 µm in all aquatic compartments, even though different sizes may induce various levels of toxicity per kg of emissions (Thornton Hampton et al. 2022; Bobori et al. 2022) and (2) the lack of a consistent degradation model for all polymers in all compartments which can distort the residence time of MiP considered in the fate factors (Louvet et al. 2026).

Lastly, it is acknowledged that plastic emissions also lead to detrimental consequences on human health (Human Health area of protection), resource availability and ecosystem services (Resources and Ecosystem services area of protection) (Kumar et al. 2021; Gabisa et al. 2023). Other damages on biodiversity are also possible through interactions with other impact categories such as Climate change, as described in Sections 1.2.4.2 and 1.2.5.1. These impact pathways and characterization models are currently under development within the MarILCA working group and will be added to this guide and its associated tool as they become available.

3.4 Interpretation

3.4.1 Analyzing the results (midpoint, damage)

Multiplying the inventory by the corresponding CFs leads to midpoint and/or endpoint (damage) impacts. These can be added to the results of a conventional LCA study, which considers other impact categories associated with other pollutants emitted over the life cycle of plastic products (e.g., climate change due to greenhouse gas emissions during production). Using LCIA methods such as GLAM, IMPACT World+ or ReCiPe can lead to damage level results, in the same units as those provided for the impact assessment of plastics, such as the PDF.yr impacts for GLAM. The use of the same endpoint indicator allows analyzing trade-offs across impact categories, and the impacts of plastic pollution can therefore be compared to the impacts of other environmental stressors based on the same endpoint unit.

The plastic footprint (at the impact level) could be used as a standalone indicator to compare different plastic alternatives based on their potential impacts on the environment. Yet, it is recommended to include the plastic footprint as part of an LCA that assesses all possible impact categories to avoid regrettable substitutions. For example, the shift from conventional to biodegradable polymers could lower the plastic footprint of a product, yet, it might increase its impacts on climate change if more greenhouse gases are emitted to produce the biodegradable alternative.

It is important to note that key differences in modeling assumptions exist between the physical effects on biota and other impact pathways, even for closely related ones, which may result in impact categories that are not directly comparable. For example, the considerations for what happens when substances reach the sediments or the global scale marine compartment are different in the physical effects on biota model compared to the USEtox model, which addresses ecotoxicity impacts. The current USEtox 2.14 version considers pollutants as being overwhelmingly diluted once they reach this global compartment and therefore models the fate of pollutants reaching the global scale as a removal mechanism from the environment. For MiP, the model was improved and the fate of MiP particles in the global scale is modeled in the same way the continental scale is modeled and thus particles are considered as affecting species at this scale also, even though the locations where concentrations can reach potentially toxic levels are extremely rare (outside of the surface depths near marine gyres, where plastic litter tends to accumulate, in the case of physical effects on biota, for example). Figure 3.3 shows the compartments included and characterized in each method, along with fate factors in each compartment.

Additionally, operationalized LCIA methods (IMPACT World+ and ReCiPe) still rely on ecotoxicity effect factors derived from HC50 (rather than HC20 as for microplastics, following GLAM recommendations). These differences may lead to potentially considerable variations in model outputs. As USEtox 3 becomes available, it is expected that IMPACT World+ will integrate this new recommendation.

3  How to assess potential biodiversity impacts of plastic emissions in LCAs

The following sections, designed as a step-by-step procedure, will allow practitioners to fully understand and apply the CFs for plastic emissions presented in this guide.

3.1 Introducing the illustrative example

To exemplify the complete process, this section uses a continuous example. In this example, a comparative LCA is conducted to assess the environmental performance of five drinking containers used in quick-service restaurant (QSR) settings:

  1. Reusable ceramic mug provided by the QSR
  2. Reusable polypropylene cup provided by the QSR
  3. Reusable polypropylene travel container brought by the customer
  4. Single-use PLA-lined paper cup provided by the QSR
  5. Single-use PE-lined paper cup provided by the QSR
  6. Single-use PET cup provided by the QSR
  7. Single-use PP cup provided by the QSR

3.2 Life cycle inventory for the foreground system

The following steps allow the computation of an inventory of plastic emissions to the environment over the life cycle of the product or service considered. At the inventory stage, details are needed on the emission country and emission compartment, the polymer type, the shape, and the size.

3.2.1 Processes and materials

As a first step in your assessment, the processes in which emissions of plastic pieces or particles can occur must be identified. Here, the focus should be on the foreground system, for reasons mentioned in the previous sections. The processes typically associated with plastic leakage are likely to be similar to the modules identified in the PFN or the PLP. For all types of plastic products, emissions are indeed likely to occur during plastic granulate manufacturing, road transportation with tire abrasion and end-of-life management, including littering. Some processes are associated with specific sectors. For example, the washing of garments and textiles during the use stage is a process in which microfibers are emitted to wastewater. The same reasoning applies to the fishing industry, which can lose fishing gear at sea, and agricultural practices involving mulch films.

For end-of-life processes, it is also important to highlight that leakage could occur via multiple pathways. Littered, uncollected and improperly disposed waste may all end up in natural environments but differ greatly in proportions. Also, waste can be managed suboptimally without necessarily leaking entirely to the environment. This distinction is relevant and justifies the need for a precise identification of processes involved in the targeted systems during the assessment. Some plastic emissions can also originate from waste exports. Indeed, waste mismanagement that occurs once exported waste lands in a country with inefficient waste management infrastructure could also be taken into consideration, if data allow this leakage to be quantified. This is the case for many high-income countries that export a significant fraction of their plastic waste to low- or middle-income countries that rely on poor waste management schemes (Kounina et al. 2024).

The identification of the processes involved in the plastic emissions should allow practitioners to also identify the polymer composition of the emissions, which constitutes relevant information since CFs are available per polymer (e.g., PET, HDPE and PP) or material (e.g., cotton and viscose). This composition of emissions is directly related to the composition of the materials assessed. Therefore, when conducting the environmental assessment of a PET food container, for example, the emissions will consist of PET.

3.2.2 Geographical location

The geographic location is a key parameter of plastic leakage and impacts. First, the location will affect emission rates of plastic into the environment. In fact, countries and regions with better waste management systems and high-efficiency wastewater treatment plants (WWTP) will lead to smaller emissions of MaP and MiP into the environment, compared to locations with poor waste management and WWTPs. As a result, the same item used in different contexts can lead to different emissions of plastic into the environment.

Second, the location also influences CFs as the fate and effects of MaP and MiP on biota can be regionalized. So far, CFs are therefore available for the following regions: Africa & Middle East, Central Asia, Europe, Latin America, North America, Northern Regions, Oceania, Southeast Asia, and a default World region, following USEtox regionalization (Figure 3.1 shows the available regions). Regional differentiation accounts for parameters such as rainfall rate, water body depth and area, species richness, area fraction, and runoff, all of which influence the environmental fate and ultimate impact of MiP.

Practitioners should therefore identify the country and geographic region in which the processes and activities under study occur, as this determines which regional leakage rate and regional CF values to apply. For instance, road transport activities taking place during distribution stages in North America should be characterized using the North America CF values and emission rates. This same logic applies to manufacturing processes, which may represent another source of MiP emissions and can occur across multiple regions within a single product system. When the location of emission cannot be determined, the World region can be used as the default.

3.2.3 Emission quantity

Following the identification of processes involved in plastic emissions throughout the life cycle of the product system under study, the quantification of these processes must be undertaken. The data used for this purpose may be labeled as emission factors, loss rates, leakage rates, or release rates, and communicates the quantity of plastic (expressed as mass) emitted by a process per unit of activity or per unit of material, for example. Note that mass is currently the designated metric of interest for quantifying plastic emissions. Particle count is also recognized as an important metric in MiP science, particularly for toxicity assessment (Thornton Hampton et al. 2022); however, mass remains the only unit used in the LCA field to date. Accordingly, any data expressed in particle numbers must be converted to mass using information on particle size and density before use in an LCA context. Lavoie et al. (2022) and Besseling et al. (2019) provide worked examples of such conversions.

Various conventions are used throughout the scientific and industrial literature to express quantities of emitted plastics. Emission factors are frequently expressed as percentages or fractions, representing the proportion of a plastic material considered to be emitted to the environment, but may also be expressed as a mass of plastic per unit of process or activity. Some of the variation observed in units and terminology is attributable to differences in what is being quantified. For instance, in the dataset published by the PFN (Plastic Footprint Guidelines 2023), some release rates (i.e., RelR) quantify direct environmental emissions, whereas others (i.e., RR) quantify emissions reaching specific environmental compartments following release and subsequent redistribution – whether through natural processes (e.g., transport of MiP from freshwater to oceans via currents) or human interventions (e.g., wastewater treatment). It is therefore essential for practitioners to carefully examine what each data source is expressing and quantifying before use. Some data sources are provided in Section 4 of this guide.

a Emissions to oceans from a t-shirt wash cycle in Canada are calculated by multiplying the reported loss rate (LR) and release rate (RRocean).

It is important to note that plastic mismanagement and plastic leakage to the environment are not synonymous, and therefore, plastic mismanagement rates cannot directly be used to determine the amount of plastic emitted to the environment. In fact, plastic mismanagement often encompasses open burning and landfilling which are not direct environmental emissions of plastic, but which may lead to environmental leakage of plastic. Data clearly identifying leakage to the environment is needed for this assessment.

A related and critical issue concerns the risk of double-counting fate when estimating quantities of plastic emissions to natural environments. As noted above, emission factors are intended to represent the mass of plastics emitted to the environment at the point of release (initial environmental compartment), while the fate modeling in the CFs accounts for redistribution between compartments. In certain methodological frameworks, however – including the PFN (Plastic Footprint Guidelines 2023) and the PLP (Peano et al. 2020) – additional redistribution rates are also provided to account for the subsequent transport of particles between environmental compartments. This creates an inconsistency when these data are combined with CFs for physical effects on biota, since CFs already fully account for the fate of MiP once released to natural compartments. Practitioners drawing on both of these data sources and the physical effects on biota CFs must therefore ensure that only emission values into the initial environmental compartment (i.e., the first environmental compartment that microplastics reach from the technosphere) – and not any further redistribution rates – are carried forward into the impact assessment.

3.2.4 Environmental compartment

Impacts are emission compartment-dependent and are available for the following emission compartments: air, agricultural soil, natural soil, river water, lake water, and sea water (see Table 3.6). In practice, the emission compartment may be straightforward to identify for some processes, but difficult to determine precisely for others.

Precise compartment assignment requires detailed information on where plastic emissions occur and, where relevant, how leaked plastic is transported before reaching the receiving environment. This level of detail is often unavailable to practitioners. Ideally, activity-specific leakage models would provide this information in a similar way as LCI datasets provide elementary flows for a wide range of substances and activities. However, plastic emissions are not yet systematically represented in these data sources. For now, practitioners therefore need to rely on complementary data sources and transparent assumptions.

At the time of writing, the most useful source of information on this matter is provided by the PFN technical modules and accompanying spreadsheets. The release rates in each module include the share of leaked plastic assigned to different receiving compartments. In the PFN data sheets used for this guide, the granularity and completeness of release rates vary by sector (see modules for complete explanation).

For example, in the Macroplastic: Packaging spreadsheet, the “Leaked to Ocean and Waterways” column provides country-specific release rates for a combined marine and freshwater compartment. This helps estimate releases to aquatic environments, but does not distinguish between sea water, river water, and lake water, and therefore does not allow direct selection of the most specific CF. In the same spreadsheet, additional generic release rates are provided for ocean and land, depending on the size of the plastic item emitted and its residual value. Larger items and items with higher residual value are associated with lower release rates. These release rates may not sum to 100%, because the remaining fraction is assumed to be removed, for example through waste picking or municipal cleaning operations. In other modules, such as the textile microfiber module, leakage is distinguished between freshwater and marine environments.

In case no precise environmental compartments are available for the system studied, as a pragmatic default, it is recommended to first apply the generic release rates for “waterways” and land of the PFN to allocate the emitted fraction between continental sea water and continental natural soil, respectively. Alternatively, depending on the country, practitioners may assume that the “Leaked to Ocean and Waterways” fraction is emitted to sea water and that the remaining emitted fraction is released to land, represented here as natural soil. This simplified compartment assignment should be documented, and fractions assumed to be removed or cleaned should not be characterized as emissions.

For MiP emissions from textile production and use, emissions to land should be assigned to agricultural soil since the release pathway occurs via sewage sludge spreading on agricultural fields. Recommendations on compartment assignment are provided in Table 3.6.

For MiP emissions from tire abrasion, it is strongly recommended to use the specific CFs available in Saadi and Boulay (2026), where applicable, as these incorporate a weighting based on the likelihood of emissions reaching different natural compartments at the continental scale. Further parameterization is possible using PFN data to adapt the emission [12.1][12.2]distribution according to watershed size and the share of driving occurring in rural, urban, and highway contexts. Because updates to the PFN methodology and data are expected, these recommendations should be revisited when new versions become available.

For compartments represented in the fate model in both continental and global versions, emissions should by default be assigned to the continental compartment. This recommendation may be revised when process-specific information justifies an alternative assignment. For instance, emissions from fishing operations conducted in open ocean waters may appropriately be assigned to the global sea water compartment. In any case, CF values between continental and global versions of a given compartment are expected to differ only marginally and are unlikely to materially affect assessment results.

3.2.5 Fragmentation

For many of the emitting processes identified previously, the plastic emissions will take the form of MAPs. Yet CFs for physical effects on biota are only available for micro-sized plastic emissions. Secondary microplastics, which originate from macroplastic fragmentation, are nevertheless thought to be the most abundant form of microplastics, more so than primary microplastics manufactured as such (Mutuku et al. 2024). This means that MAPs undergo significant fragmentation under the forces encountered in the environment. It is therefore generally accepted that a significant proportion of the MAPs that end up in natural environments will fragment over time, especially over long time horizons.

On this matter, a few recommendations can be put forward, based on a peer-reviewed degradation and fragmentation model (Tang et al., submitted). The idea is simply to use a percentage value (%) to represent the proportion of MAP emissions that will fragment and should thus be characterized using the CFs for physical effects on biota. These fragmentation percentages vary by material type (i.e. rigid or flexible) and emission compartment (i.e. topsoil, beach, or water surface). The rationale is that a flexible material likely has a larger surface-area-to-volume ratio than a rigid polymer, making it more susceptible to fragmentation. As for emission compartments, UV intensity and mechanical forces influence the fragmentation observed, and thus the variable rates across compartments. Table 3.9 summarizes the recommendations for the various contexts.

Additionally, the remaining fraction of plastic that is not assumed to fragment cannot be characterized using the physical effects on biota CFs and should therefore remain uncharacterized, in the absence of pertinent indicators expressing its potential contribution to damages on biodiversity.

3.2.6 Shape

The shape of emitted particles is generally determined by the type of product. Detailed recommendations on this matter, drawn from Corella-Puertas et al. (2023), are provided in this guide and summarized in Table 3.12, as follows: fragments from film-like materials (wrappings, bags, etc.) should be characterized as film fragments; particles from fiber-containing materials (textiles, ropes, etc.) should be treated as fibers; and particles originating from foams or foam-like materials, as well as microbeads, should be considered spherical. For particles of unknown or unspecified shape, the default assumption is also spherical; a choice supported by evidence that plastic fragmentation tends to produce increasingly rounded particles over time (Alkema et al. 2022).

3.2.7 Size

CFs expressing damage to biodiversity from MIP emissions span up to 6 orders of magnitude across the 1–5000 µm size range (Louvet et al. 2026). Particle size is therefore a critical parameter in LCA, as the choice of size class can significantly influence results. For primary MIP; particles manufactured and released directly at the micro scale; size characterization is generally straightforward, as their dimensions are defined at the point of production and should be known to the practitioner.

Fragmentation breaks larger plastic pieces into smaller debris, which can eventually become part of what is referred to as micro- or nano-plastics (< 5 mm). The size distribution of microplastics after fragmentation from macro-sized litter is deeply uncertain. Because CFs can vary by up to 6 orders of magnitude depending on emitted particle size (Louvet et al. 2026), this information is crucial for precisely and rigorously assessing the potential damages on biodiversity caused by emissions of plastic litter in natural environments.

In this context, several avenues may shed light on the matter. Based on the commonness of microplastic sizes found in the environment and in industry, Corella-Puertas et al. (2023) proposes default sizes for the various shapes of microplastics. For generic plastic materials (MAPs) (e.g. buoys, kettles, and toys) that must be characterized as microplastics in order to use the physical effects on biota CFs, the recommended size class is 1000 µm or 5000 µm, since large particles will likely dominate the fragmented mass during the early stages of fragmentation (Egger et al. 2020; Kaandorp et al. 2021; Ueda et al. 2025). This also matches the recommendations of Corella-Puertas et al. (2023) for fragments of unspecified shape. Table 3.14 lays out this information.

For clarity, it should be noted that fiber and film fragment sizes refer to their diameter and thickness, respectively; not to particle length or overall dimensions. These dimensional properties govern environmental fate and therefore constitute the primary characteristics of interest in this analysis. For spheres or unknown-shape fragments, the diameter represents the dimension of interest.

3.2.8 Limits of the inventory of plastic emissions

Limits and weaknesses of plastic emission inventories exist and are worth highlighting, since they can significantly influence the results of the assessment. Because CFs for physical effects on biota are quite large, especially for large particle sizes and high-density polymers, a small variation in the quantities of emitted plastics can shift the conclusions of a study.

Drawing on experience from previous work estimating plastic emissions to natural environments, the most pressing limit of the inventory concerns the fragmentation of MAPs into secondary microplastics, which can then be characterized using the physical effects on biota CFs. The wide range of assumptions made on this question in the past; 10%, 50%, and 100% (Corella-Puertas et al. 2023; Plastic Footprint Guidelines 2023); 0.5% (Erradhouani et al. 2026); 100% (Louvet et al. 2026); and 4%, 12%, and 62% (Saadi et al. 2025); reveals the substantial unknowns associated with this parameter, especially given the variety of product types, environmental conditions, and polymer chemistries involved. We therefore recommend always including a sensitivity analysis on the value of this parameter.

A further limitation concerns the focus on the foreground system. Since plastic emission inventories are not currently assessed in LCI databases, background systems will essentially never include plastic emissions, even though in reality many occur in background packaging and oceanic freight activities, for example.

Most limits faced during the inventory stage will likely originate from data availability. Some sectors may be unable to find accurate and reliable data on the plastic emissions occurring throughout their supply chain. Because data may come from many sources, methodologies and assumptions may also differ, inducing considerable uncertainty in the assessment. Furthermore, obtaining uncertainty estimates (e.g. confidence intervals) for inventory emission factors is mostly impossible given the current state of knowledge in the field.

Specific limitations are also linked to particular data sources or methodologies. For example, in low-income countries (LICs) and middle-income countries (MICs), open dumps are the de facto treatment for waste disposal. Yet some methodologies, such as the PFN (Plastic Footprint Guidelines 2023) and the PLP (Peano et al. 2020), consider this end-of-life treatment as waste mismanagement and apply a 100% leakage to litter taking this path. While there is consensus that open dumps are far from optimal, this approach stands in stark contrast to other scientific studies, which instead suggest leakage rates of 0.04% (Cottom et al. 2024), 5% (Schwarz et al. 2023), and 25% (OECD 2022). These values, although widely scattered, all differ significantly from the 100% assumed by the PLP (Peano et al. 2020) and the PFN (Plastic Footprint Guidelines 2023).

Similarly, potential plastic emissions resulting from plastic waste exports are not currently accounted for directly by the emission factors provided in the PFN (Plastic Footprint Guidelines 2023). The share of plastic waste exported is provided, but linking these exports to specific destination countries and assigning the corresponding emission factors of waste treated there requires additional data and work by the practitioner. Quantifying emissions from plastic waste exports is nonetheless possible. To do so, practitioners can rely on the waste-export shares given in the PFN data for the country where the end-of-life of their product system occurs. Identifying the destination countries would be ideal; alternatively, practitioners may use the average waste-mismanagement percentages for low- and middle-income countries provided in the PLP data, and multiply the two values to estimate the resulting emission factors. The usual release step is then followed as normal to identify the natural compartments where emissions occur.

The limited transparency of data provided by some initiatives makes it difficult to modify emission factors to reflect the specific context of a study, or to integrate partial data the practitioner may already have; ultimately limiting their control over the quality and representativeness of their assessment.

On the operational side, practitioners may also face practical limitations in the time needed to account for contributions from physical effects on biota. Since the process described in the previous sections is not yet fully integrated into databases and software the way other impact categories are, additional steps are necessary to incorporate these contributions into the tables and figures communicating the results. This can prove particularly cumbersome when multiple products, scenarios, and sensitivity analyses are carried out over the course of the assessment.

3.3 Life cycle impact assessment (LCIA)

3.3.1 Physical effects on biota

The CFs for physical effects on biota express the potential detrimental impacts on species communities associated with emissions of MIP to natural environments. They account for both the potential effects of MIP on exposed organisms and the residence time of MIP in the environmental compartments through which particles may be transported. They are available on two time scales, representing short- and long-term time-horizon perspectives, similar to the Climate change short-term and long-term indicators available in the IMPACT World+ methodology. Following the same approach as Climate change, the short-term physical effects on biota CFs quantify the potential impacts occurring during the 0–100 year time horizon, while the long-term CFs quantify those occurring beyond 100 years.

As indicated by the name of the impact category, the current CFs account only for physical effects. This reflects the way the effect factors were developed: they rely on ecotoxicological data from experiments using virgin MIP, in order to isolate the physical effects of plastic particles on organisms, without including potential effects from additives or other chemicals present in plastic product matrices (Lavoie et al. 2022; Corella-Puertas et al. 2022, 2023; Saadi et al. 2025; Louvet et al. 2026; Saadi and Boulay 2026).

Chemical substances added to plastic products to modify or enhance their properties, such as additives, are therefore not represented in the physical effects on biota pathway. When relevant, and when sufficient inventory and substance-specific data are available, these chemical contributions should instead be considered through existing ecotoxicity impact pathways. Similarly, the fate modelling focuses on the intrinsic properties of the polymer particles; for example, degradation and fragmentation behaviour; and does not explicitly account for formulation-specific effects such as additional stability induced by additives. Section 1.2.3 and discusses the distinction between physical effects on biota and ecotoxicity, and proposes approaches for addressing this issue in practice.

The physical effects considered in this pathway include entanglement, ingestion, and adherence. These mechanisms may lead to adverse outcomes such as mortality, reduced growth, reduced feeding, or reduced reproduction.

The CFs are provided for 14 materials, 5 sizes, 4 shapes, 9 environmental compartments, and 2 scales. These are listed in Table 3.18, and the CFs themselves are available here.

3.3.1.1 Units for various modeling frameworks and LCIA methodologies

Various methodologies are available to practitioners for the LCIA step of their assessment. These methodologies combine different models across a number of impact categories and sometimes also imply a series of value choices in their modeling assumptions (e.g. the time horizons considered). LCIA methods are generally either midpoint- or damage-oriented, though some cover both (Bulle et al. 2019). The physical effects on biota impact category provides CFs at both midpoint and damage levels and is implemented in IMPACT World+ (Bulle et al. 2019), one of the most widely used LCIA methodologies available. Additionally, to facilitate their integration with diverse LCIA methodologies, the physical effects on biota CFs are provided in different versions, using different units.

3.3.1.2 Limits of the model

Models are imperfect representations of real-world processes. The modeling involved in the development of the CFs is no exception and thus carries many limitations. One of the main limitations stems from data availability, especially for the polymer degradation rates used in developing the fate factors. Quality experiments are scarce, so obtaining enough data to accurately represent the various mechanisms and environmental stresses that govern degradation rates across the range of existing polymers is a significant challenge given current knowledge [Tang, submitted].

TipContinuous example: impact assessment

Following the inventory compiled in the previous section, the LCI results are now translated into LCIA indicator results. The approach is straightforward: the emitted quantities of MIP are multiplied by the appropriate CF, following the usual LCIA procedure. In the continuous example, the final inventories give quantities of emitted MIP for a given polymer, shape, and size, and for two environmental compartments. Each quantity is multiplied by the appropriate CF; at midpoint or damage level, depending on the practitioner’s needs; to obtain its contribution to the impact indicator. The ocean and land scores are then summed to give a final impact score.

3.3.2 Limits of the impact assessment of plastic emissions

Models are incomplete representations of reality and therefore carry numerous limitations. For the impact assessment of plastic emissions specifically, several can be identified. First, the physical effects on biota CFs are for now only available for MIP-sized plastic particles. Combined with the uncertainty surrounding environmental fragmentation processes and the absence of CFs for MAP, this means that significant fractions of the plastic litter emitted to the environment may go unaccounted for at the impact assessment step. This results in a systematic underestimation of impacts and creates a mass-balance inconsistency: the total mass of plastic recorded in the life cycle inventory is not fully characterized at the impact assessment level, as MAPs are effectively assigned a zero-impact weight by default.

Second, current model assumptions focus on the physical effects on biota, which, as the impact category name suggests, are limited to the physical effects on organisms. This implies that plastic pieces leaking to the environment are assumed to be composed of 100% polymer chains, rather than a mix of polymer and the chemical additives that complement the functionality of real plastic products on the market. This is a significant shortcoming, since it means the chemical toxicity of such substances; and of other components such as un-polymerized monomers; is currently unaccounted for. This may, however, matter less than it sounds for the impact assessment of plastic litter, because the relative mass of potential additives currently treated as polymer material may not be as harmful to ecosystems as is often assumed (Lavoie et al. 2022; Corella-Puertas et al. 2023; Louvet et al. 2026).

Third, regarding the modeling of the physical effects on biota, many limitations are reported in the papers describing the development of the model’s various components. They include, but are not limited to: (1) the use of a single generic effect factor to represent the physical effect of particles ranging from 0.001 µm to 5000 µm in all aquatic compartments, even though different sizes induce different levels of toxicity per kg of emissions (Thornton Hampton et al. 2022; Bobori et al. 2022) and impacts may vary with the species communities of different ecosystems; and (2) the lack of a consistent degradation model across all polymers and compartments, which can distort the residence time of MIP considered in the fate factors (Louvet et al. 2026).

Finally, it is crucial to remember that the cause-effect chains developed so far address only the potential damages on biodiversity (Ecosystem Quality area of protection) generated by ingestion, adherence, entanglement, and other physical effects. Yet it is widely acknowledged that plastic emissions also have detrimental consequences for human health (Human Health area of protection) and for resource availability and ecosystem services (Resources and Ecosystem Services area of protection) (Kumar et al. 2021; Gabisa et al. 2023). Further damages on biodiversity are also possible through interactions with other impact categories such as Climate change, as described in Section 1.2.4.2. The LCA community recognizes this, and the MarILCA framework identifies the development of cause-effect models linking plastic emissions to these endpoints as part of the work ahead (Woods et al. 2021). Other impact categories and areas of protection may therefore not reveal any impact contributions from plastic emissions for now, but these are simply uncharacterized, rather than nonexistent.

3.3.3 Other considerations for the impact assessment of plastic products

In practice, the distinction between the two impact pathways, physical effects on biota and ecotoxicity, is also genuinely important. In most cases, practitioners may not have access to robust data on the additive composition of the plastic products under study, since this information is scarce and barely accessible. In such contexts, the recommendation is to consider the full mass of plastic as virgin (Approach A, Figure 3.2). This can be seen as a conservative approach, since the impact contributions from the polymer fraction greatly surpass those from additives (Lecointre et al. 2025). When information on additive content is available, separating the polymer and additive fractions and characterizing each independently, using the available CFs for physical effects on biota and ecotoxicity, respectively, is an improved yet still simple way of handling the issue (Approach B, Figure 3.2). This approach remains imperfect, as it assumes the effect, fate, and exposure of both fractions are independent once the plastic product is emitted to the environment, which is a significant assumption. It is also possible to assess the fate of additives in the polymer matrix more precisely, using tools such as the Food Contact Material tool in USEtox 3.0 (Approach C, Figure 3.2). This last approach offers a more robust and precise assessment of the fate of additives, but still carries shortcomings such as time constraints and data availability.

3.4 Interpretation

3.4.1 Analyzing the results (midpoint, damage)

Key differences in modeling assumptions exist between the physical effects on biota pathway and others, even closely related ones. For example, the treatment of substances that reach the global-scale marine compartment differs between the physical effects on biota model and the USEtox model, which addresses ecotoxicity impacts. The current USEtox 2.14 version treats pollutants as overwhelmingly diluted once they reach this global compartment, and therefore models their fate at the global scale as a removal mechanism from the environment. For MIP, the model was improved: the fate of MIP particles at the global scale is modeled in the same way as at the continental scale, so particles are considered to affect species at this scale too, even though the locations where concentrations can reach potentially toxic levels are extremely rare (in the case of physical effects on biota, outside the near-surface depths around marine gyres, where plastic litter tends to accumulate). This can lead to considerable variation in model outputs.

Similarly, the time horizons over which impacts are integrated can vary by impact category, which can distort the results; especially for damage-level indicators, which aggregate many inconsistent models into a single value. For example, the USEtox model, used as the consensus approach for the ecotoxicity and human toxicity impact categories, integrates potential impacts until a steady state is reached, which in practice means over an infinite time horizon. For Climate change, impacts are integrated over 100 years (short-term) or 500 years (long-term). These considerable differences generate significant discrepancies in output. Such limitations are present throughout LCIA methodologies and are not specific to the physical effects on biota framework.

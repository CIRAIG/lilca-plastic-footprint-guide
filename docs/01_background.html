1  Background

1.1 Plastics: functional benefits and environmental concerns

Plastics have become deeply embedded in modern economies due to their versatility, low density, durability, barrier properties, and relatively low production costs. These properties make them useful across a wide range of applications, including packaging, healthcare, construction, transportation, and electronics. In some systems, plastics can contribute to material efficiency, product protection, reduced transport burdens, or longer service life. These benefits should be acknowledged, because the environmental relevance of plastic products depends on the function delivered and on the full life cycle considered. Reflecting their widespread use, cumulative primary plastic production was estimated to have reached approximately 9.5 billion tonnes by 2019 (Geyer et al. 2017), and annual production continues to increase (Figure 1.1).

At the same time, the scale of plastic production and use has resulted in growing plastic stocks within the technosphere and increasing waste flows requiring end-of-life management. Yet only a limited fraction of plastic waste is successfully recycled globally, commonly estimated at 9.3% (Figure 1.2). As a result, substantial quantities of plastic waste are landfilled, incinerated, mismanaged, or released to natural environments. In an LCA context, the key issue is therefore not only the generation of plastic waste, but the fraction of plastic flows that may leave the technosphere and become plastic emissions to environmental compartments.

Global assessments provide an indication of both the scale and the sources of plastic leakage to the environment. OECD estimates suggest that around 22 Mt of plastics entered natural environments in 2019, reflecting the combined effects of high plastic consumption and insufficient waste management systems (Figure 1.3). Most of these emissions were macroplastics (MAP), representing approximately 19.4 Mt, primarily associated with inadequate waste collection and disposal practices. Additional contributions arose from littering, and marine-based activities. Although smaller in quantity, microplastics (MIP, typically defined as particles ranging from 1 µm to 5 mm) represented a significant fraction of total leakage and were mainly generated through dust, tyre abrasion, wastewater sludge, and losses of industrial plastic pellets (OECD 2022).

Once released into terrestrial, freshwater, or marine ecosystems, plastics can generate significant ecological impacts (Figure 1.4). Larger plastic debris, termed macroplastics (MAP), can be ingested by fauna or cause entanglement, leading to injury, impaired mobility, reduced feeding capacity, or mortality in birds, mammals, fish, reptiles or other taxa (Beaumont et al. 2019). Beyond these visible impacts, fragmentation processes driven by UV radiation, mechanical abrasion, and biological activity progressively generate smaller particles. These microplastics and nanoplastics (<1 µm) may either be intentionally produced at small sizes (primary microplastics) or result from the breakdown of larger items (secondary microplastics).

As particle size decreases, the spectrum of potentially exposed organisms broadens. Micro- (MIP) and nanoplastics can be ingested by a wide range of species, including zooplankton, benthic invertebrates, and other organisms at the base of trophic networks. In aquatic environments, exposure may occur through feeding, filtration, or ventilation processes (Wright et al. 2013; Besseling et al. 2015). Experimental evidence also indicates that micro- and nanoplastics can affect photoautotrophic organisms, including through effects on growth, photosynthetic activity, and oxidative stress, although responses vary depending on particle size, shape, polymer type, concentration, and exposure conditions (Zhang et al. 2017; Bhattacharya et al. 2010; Sjollema et al. 2016). Because these organisms contribute to primary production, nutrient cycling, and trophic transfer, effects at lower trophic levels may propagate through food webs and affect ecosystem functioning (Galloway et al. 2017; De Sá et al. 2018).

Given the scale of plastic production, the persistence of materials in the environment, and the diversity of exposure pathways and affected taxa, these impacts must be systematically considered when evaluating the environmental performance of products and services. Integrating Ecosystem Quality indicators related to plastic and microplastic emissions in LCA is therefore essential to ensure a more comprehensive and ecologically meaningful sustainability assessment.

1.2 History of the plastic litter issue in LCA

1.2.1 Inventory data developments

The Plastic Leak Project (Peano et al. 2020) was the first science-based initiative aiming at developing inventory models allowing the quantification of plastic emissions towards the environment from various processes and activities. Their Sectorial Guidance Generic data allowed for the quantification of MIP or MAP leakage associated with four main processes (Peano et al. 2020):

  • Pellet production (MIPs),
  • Tire abrasion during transport (MIPs),
  • Microfibers releases from textile washing (MIPs), and
  • Losses occurring at the end-of-life through waste mismanagement or littering (MAPs) .

Building on this initiative, the Plastic Footprint Network (PFN) was launched in 2023 with a broader scope. While the Plastic Leak Project focused primarily on inventory models for quantifying plastic emissions, the PFN seek to connect plastic emissions inventories with impact assessment within a single cohesive framework. For the impact assessment step, the PFN relies on MarILCA and therefore mainly focuses its efforts on the development of the inventory step. In this regard, several PFN technical modules have been developed to address key sources of plastic leakage across the life cycle, including packaging, textiles, and textiles fibers, tires, fishing, fishing gear, agricultural plastics, and leakage associated with waste exports.

Other assessments provide overviews of global emissions without aiming to allocate or scale these emissions to specific product systems or activities (Cottom et al. 2024; Han et al. 2026). Such studies are useful for identifying major sources and pathways of plastic leakage on a global scale, but they do not provide the activity-based emission factors or modelling structure required for assessments at the product or service scale.

1.2.2 Available CFs for LCIA

1.2.2.1 Physical effects on biota (microplastics)

The following overview focuses on the development of CFs representing physical effects on biota within the MarILCA framework. MarILCA currently provides one of the most operational approaches in LCIA for quantifying potential impacts associated with MIP emissions to the environment.

The development of these CFs responded to a major gap in standard LCA practice. Before the launch of the MarILCA working group, plastic leakage from product systems, and the environmental consequences associated with such emissions, were generally not accounted for. Commonly used inventories did not include dedicated flows for identifying and quantifying plastic releases to environmental compartments, while LCIA methods did not provide CFs to characterize their potential impacts. This gap was explicitly highlighted by the Medellin Declaration on Marine Litter in Life Cycle Assessment and Management, which called for the integration of plastic litter issues into both inventory modelling and impact assessment (Sonnemann and Valdivia 2017). It was subsequently addressed through the creation of the MarILCA working group in 2018.

Within this context, a first major milestone was the development of a structured methodological framework to assess the potential impacts of plastic litter emissions (Figure 1.6). Woods et al. (2021) formalized this framework and established a clear research agenda, identifying the key modelling components required to derive CFs. With this conceptual foundation in place, subsequent work focused on progressively operationalizing the different building blocks needed to quantify physical effects on biota.

The operationalization of this framework began in 2022, the first of these building blocks was published, introducing a generic effect factor to account for physical effects on biota (Lavoie et al. 2022). Building on this contribution, Corella-Puertas et al. (2022) proposed an initial set of simplified CFs to estimate potential impacts on Ecosystem Quality resulting from emissions of expanded polystyrene and tire and road wear particles in aquatic environments. At that stage, the fate model was deliberately simplified, considering only degradation and sedimentation processes and relying on uncertain slow-to-fast degradation rates. Further methodological refinements followed, extending the framework to additional polymers and particle shapes. These developments culminated in the first official release of CFs in Corella-Puertas et al. (2023), which presented revised fate and effect factors.

Building on the 2023 release, subsequent developments broadened the model scope and improved its robustness. Saadi et al. (2025) incorporated impacts occurring in sediments, which had previously been omitted. This refinement led to substantial increases in CF values, particularly for high-density polymers whose longer residence times in sediments, combined with slow degradation processes, amplify potential impacts (Saadi et al. 2025). The same work also introduced CFs for semi-synthetic and cellulosic-based natural fibers. In early 2026, another significant update aligned the fate modelling approach with the USEtox consensus framework for toxicity and ecotoxicity impact categories (Rosenbaum et al. 2008; Fantke et al. 2021), integrating the work of Quik et al. (2024). At that stage, the revised model was applicable to spherical MIPs only. Finally, in late 2026, a consolidated and comprehensive version of the CFs was released, extending coverage to the three particle shapes identified in earlier stages of the research, namely spheres, fibers, and films, while also expanding the range of polymers and materials considered (Saadi et al., submitted).

Taken together, these CFs aim to quantitatively represent the potential detrimental physical effects of MIP emissions to natural environments on biodiversity, expressed under the area of protection Ecosystem Quality. A central feature of the framework is the explicit consideration of residence time across environmental compartments, which captures how transport, degradation, sedimentation, and accumulation influence the duration and magnitude of potential impact. Although CFs are provided in different units depending on their alignment with specific LCIA methodologies, the underlying objective remains consistent: to capture, in an operational and scientifically grounded manner, the potential physical effects of MIPs on biota. Despite these advances, important uncertainties remain, particularly regarding degradation dynamics, species sensitivity data, and the scaling of organism-level effects to biodiversity damage metrics.

1.2.2.2 Entanglement impacts from fishing gear (MAPs)

Spatially explicit CFs to quantify potential impacts on Ecosystem Quality from entanglement-prone MAP debris, particularly lost fishing gear, have recently been developed by Høiberg et al. (2024). These CFs are provided at the scale of exclusive economic zones (EEZs) and as country-level averages, representing a significant advancement in the spatial differentiation of MAP impacts. However, their methodological framework is not fully aligned with the modelling approach used in MarILCA for MIP emissions, which limits their direct integration within a unified impact assessment framework.

First, the fate modelling underlying Høiberg et al. (2024) does not explicitly quantify the residence time of debris across environmental compartments in a manner consistent with multimedia fate modelling approaches typically applied in LCIA. The period during which debris may remain available to entangle organisms must therefore be assessed separately, which can be particularly challenging for non-experts. As a result, temporal dynamics are not treated in a way that is directly comparable to MIP fate modelling in Corella-Puertas et al. (2023), Saadi et al. (2025), or Louvet et al. (2026). Second, the effect modelling does not rely on endpoints such as mortality, impaired reproduction, or reduced growth, which are more directly aligned with the consensus toxicity framework used in USEtox (Rosenbaum et al. 2008). Instead, the approach estimates the fraction of potentially affected species based on their susceptibility to interacting with entanglement-prone MAP debris. This may lead to conservative estimates of potential impact, since not all interactions necessarily translate into ecologically relevant damage at the population level.

Finally, the scope of the CFs developed by Høiberg et al. (2024) is restricted to debris with entanglement-prone geometries, such as fishing nets and lines. This limits their applicability to a specific subset of MAP items and makes it difficult to systematically link these CFs to the diverse range of product shapes encountered in product-level LCAs. Consequently, while these CFs represent a valuable step toward integrating MAP entanglement impacts into LCIA, further methodological harmonisation would be required to ensure consistency with MIP impact pathways.

Despite these limitations, these CFs have been tested in combination with CFs for physical effects on biota from MIP emissions in a fishing industry case study. The results showed that MAP entanglement impacts can be highly relevant for Ecosystem Quality, particularly for anchoveta fishing, where they dominated the results under the assumptions tested. For cod fishing, entanglement was also an important contributor, although it was not necessarily the dominant one when other impact categories were included. The results were highly sensitive to the assumed time horizon over which debris remained capable of causing entanglement impacts, which remains a major source of uncertainty.

1.2.3 Additive coverage in LCA

While CFs representing the environmental impacts of plastic emissions have been developed and continue to evolve, most available developments focus on physical impacts and therefore exclude chemical impacts associated with the specific composition of products (Woods et al. 2021; Lavoie et al. 2022; Corella-Puertas et al. 2023; Saadi et al. 2025; Louvet et al. 2026). Everyday plastic products can contain non-negligible amounts of chemical additives used to modify or enhance product properties, including plasticizers, flame retardants, pigments, antistatic agents, biocides, and light stabilizers (Vincoff et al. 2024; Monclús et al. 2025). This issue has been recognized within the MarILCA framework but is not addressed by the CFs introduced in the previous section. The physical effects on biota pathway characterizes plastic particles through their polymer fraction and does not account for formulation-specific additives. By contrast, chemical effects associated with additives would need to be represented through ecotoxicity-related modelling, provided that the identity, amount, release, fate, and toxicity of the relevant substances can be specified (Woods et al. 2021; Lavoie et al. 2022; Corella-Puertas et al. 2023). However, as reported by Logan et al. (2024), additive coverage in LCI databases remains highly limited, partly because exact formulations are often unavailable due to industrial confidentiality and competitiveness concerns Law et al. (2024).

Current assessments suggest that most potential impacts on Ecosystem Quality attributable to plastic emissions are associated with the polymer fraction rather than with additive substances leaching from plastic items (Lavoie et al., 202X). These results indicate that the main contributors to Ecosystem Quality impacts may already be captured by the physical effects on biota CFs. However, when CFs are applied to the total mass of a plastic item rather than only to its polymer fraction, impacts may be slightly overestimated because additives are implicitly treated as part of the characterized polymer mass, for which the CFs are greater than additives. In practice, however, information on the additive composition of plastic items remains too sparse to support precise and systematic impact assessment with the current state of knowledge.

1.2.4 Work in progress CFs for LCIA

1.2.4.1 Human toxicity non-cancer (MIP)

MIP emissions to natural environments have the potential to generate impacts on human health through both the inhalation of airborne MIPs and the ingestion of contaminated food and beverages (Lamichhane et al. 2023; Azoulay et al. 2019). Indeed, plants and animals consumed by humans, such as rice, fish, and seafood have been shown to contain non-negligible amounts of MIPs (Dessì et al., 2021; Ribeiro et al., 2020; Anastasopoulou et al., 2018). MIPs have also been detected in multiple human tissues, demonstrating that they can enter the human body, bypass protective barriers, and potentially lead to deleterious health effects (Koelmans et al. 2022; Magana-Arachchi and Wanigatunge 2023).

The impact pathway currently under development considers the distribution of MIPs in the environment after emission, as well as their bioconcentration and bioaccumulation in food. Human exposure, based on region-average diets, is then combined with non-cancer toxicological effects derived from mammalian data, thereby providing a first set of CFs, which are expected to be available in late 2026.

1.2.4.2 Marine carbon pump (MIP)

This model is currently under development, and the first CFs should be available in 2027.

1.2.4.3 Ingestion of micro- and macroplastics

New developments have also been proposed for combined MIP and MAP ingestion. Indeed, Marhoon et al. (2026) addressed another physical impact pathway associated with plastic emissions: ingestion by marine species, which had already been partially considered in previous work for MIP. The authors developed a global EF to quantify the fraction of marine air-breathing vertebrate species potentially affected by macro- and microplastic ingestion. The model covers marine mammals, seabirds, and sea turtles, and is based on field observations of plastic ingestion prevalence in stranded and bycaught individuals across several ocean basins. Species-specific exposure was estimated using spatially explicit plastic concentration data for floating debris at the ocean surface, combined with species range information. These data were then used to derive dose-response relationships and construct a field-based SSD (species sensitivity distribution).

The resulting EF is expressed in PAF·km³/kg and represents the increase in the fraction of potentially affected species per unit increase in plastic concentration in marine surface water. Marhoon et al. (2026) estimated an HC20EC10 of 51.7 kg/km³, corresponding to an EF of 3.87 × 10⁻³ PAF·km³/kg. This value is approximately three orders of magnitude higher than the EF used for MIP physical effects on biota in Corella-Puertas et al. (2023), which was subsequently applied or further developed in later CF developments such as Saadi et al. (2025) and Louvet et al. (2026). This difference partly reflects the broader scope of the ingestion EF, which covers both macro- and microplastics and is derived from field-based ingestion prevalence data, whereas earlier MIP effect modelling is based on laboratory-derived evidence and combines several physical effect mechanisms rather than treating ingestion as a distinct pathway.

This development is important because it provides a first operational basis for representing plastic ingestion as a specific physical impact pathway in LCIA, including for MAP, for which ingestion impacts had previously not been characterized. However, the EF should be interpreted as an intermediate and generic estimate. It does not quantify observed biological damage following ingestion, but instead assumes that ingestion may lead to lethal or sub-lethal physical effects. It is also generic with respect to polymer type, particle shape, and plastic size, and currently focuses only on marine air-breathing vertebrates exposed to floating debris. Marhoon et al. therefore recommend coupling this EF with appropriate fate modelling before full integration into LCIA, following the broader framework proposed for plastic litter impact assessment.

1.3 Future directions for CF development

1.3.1 Additional disruptions of the carbon cycle

MIP emissions to natural environments have been shown to interfere with carbon cycling through multiple mechanisms, extending beyond the biological carbon pump disruption. In particular, adverse effects on phytoplankton, zooplankton, and soil biota communities may alter primary production, trophic interactions, microbial respiration processes, and organic carbon turnover, thereby affecting carbon sequestration (Zhu et al. 2025). Such ecological perturbations could translate into measurable shifts in both marine and terrestrial carbon dynamics, although their magnitude and direction remain context-dependent and insufficiently quantified for operational integration into LCIA.

Zhu et al. (2025) further identify additional mechanisms that may appear secondary when considered individually, but could cumulatively contribute to broader changes in carbon dynamics. Despite their potential relevance at larger spatial and temporal scales, these pathways are not currently represented in LCIA models. Their integration would require dedicated fate, exposure, and effect modelling frameworks, together with corresponding CFs. Progress in this direction will depend on the availability of sufficiently robust empirical datasets to support quantitative relationships between MIP exposure, ecological responses, and carbon cycle feedbacks.
